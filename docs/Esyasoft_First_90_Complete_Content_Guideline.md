# ESYASOFT --- FIRST 90

## Complete Content & Implementation Guideline

**Audience:** Every new joiner at Esyasoft --- not only Graduate
Engineer Trainees.

**Purpose:** This is the implementation-ready content specification for
the First 90 website. It replaces the fragmented Company / Business /
Workplace / Policy / Help navigation with four useful destinations:
**START HERE / ESYASOFT / HOW WE WORK / FIRST 90**.

> The site should feel like a thoughtful colleague helping a new
> employee find their feet --- not like an HR portal, corporate
> brochure, training LMS, dashboard, or game.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 1. GLOBAL IMPLEMENTATION RULES

## Main navigation

Use exactly:

``` text
ESYASOFT / FIRST 90

START HERE     ESYASOFT     HOW WE WORK     FIRST 90

                                      Search     GRID
```

Remove separate top-level navigation for Business, Workplace, Policy
Library and Help. Their useful content is consolidated into the four
sections.

## Tone

Warm, clear, practical, respectful and detailed.

Do not cut useful information merely to make pages shorter. Do not add
filler merely to make them longer.

Avoid corporate/gamified copy such as "unlock your potential", "level
up", "missions", "supercharge", "activate", "XP", or "rewards".

Prefer sentences such as:

> You do not need to know everything on Day 1.

> If you are unsure, ask.

> Your manager, teammates, HR and IT are part of your onboarding too.

> Use the current official policy when you need the exact rule.

## Source labels

Use four labels where useful:

-   **OFFICIAL** --- approved internal policy/handbook/process.
-   **COMPANY CONTEXT** --- public Esyasoft material.
-   **ONBOARDING GUIDANCE** --- practical advice written for this guide.
-   **PROGRAM / ROLE SPECIFIC** --- applies only to a particular
    programme, role, team or location.

Never invent formal rules, approval chains, contacts, working hours,
attendance rules, security procedures, reimbursement amounts,
performance triggers, PIP rules, reporting structures or other internal
policy details that are not available.

Where exact information is unavailable, give safe general guidance and
point to the current official source.

# 3. START HERE

## Hero

**Eyebrow:** START HERE

**Heading:** Your practical first steps.

**Body:**

> There is a lot to take in when you join a new company. Start with what
> is relevant to you today. The sections below cover the things most new
> joiners need first: getting ready, getting settled, understanding who
> to talk to, and finding the systems and information you will use.

## Before You Join

If you have not started yet, you do not need to prepare for everything.

Focus on knowing the basics: - where and when you are expected to
report, - who your joining or HR contact is, - what documents or
information you need to provide, - whether any equipment or access
arrangements have been communicated, - whether there are
location-specific instructions, - and where you can find the latest
official joining information.

Your offer, joining communication and official HR/Admin communication
should always take priority over this guide.

### Checklist

-   Read your joining communication carefully.
-   Save the contact details provided by HR or your joining coordinator.
-   Note your joining date, reporting location and reporting
    instructions.
-   Complete requested forms/documentation.
-   Keep required identification and employment documents accessible.
-   Check whether laptop, email, access card or other equipment
    arrangements have been communicated.
-   If relocating/travelling for a programme, read the latest official
    travel/accommodation communication.
-   Do not worry about learning the entire company before Day 1.

> You are allowed to arrive without knowing everything. Curiosity is
> more useful than trying to memorise the company website before you
> start.

## First Day

Your first day is mainly about orientation and connection.

You are not expected to understand the entire organisation, technology
stack, product, or your role in a few hours.

Try to leave the day knowing: 1. who you report to, 2. who your
immediate teammates are, 3. what your first responsibilities are, 4.
which systems you need access to, 5. where official information lives,
6. how your team communicates, 7. what you should focus on next.

### First-day checklist

-   [ ] Meet your manager/reporting contact.
-   [ ] Understand what your immediate team does.
-   [ ] Know what you are expected to focus on first.
-   [ ] Meet the people you will work with most closely.
-   [ ] Confirm access to relevant systems.
-   [ ] Understand primary team communication channels.
-   [ ] Find important company resources and policies.
-   [ ] Understand who to approach when blocked.
-   [ ] Ask what you should read or understand first.
-   [ ] Write down questions.

### Questions worth asking

-   What should I focus on during my first week?
-   Who should I approach when I need help?
-   Where does the team keep documentation?
-   Which communication channel should I use for what?
-   What meetings should I attend?
-   Is there anything I should read before my first task?
-   What would you like me to understand by the end of my first month?

## First Week

The first week is about building context.

### Day 1 --- CONNECT

Meet people and understand where you fit.

### Day 2 --- UNDERSTAND

Learn what your team does and why it matters.

### Day 3 --- OBSERVE

Watch how communication, meetings, documentation and decisions happen.

### Day 4 --- EXPLORE

Read systems, products, processes or technical material relevant to your
role.

### Day 5 --- REFLECT

Write down: - what you understand, - what is still unclear, - who you
need to speak to, - what you want to learn next.

### First-week outcome

> By the end of your first week, aim to explain: "This is what my team
> does, this is where my work fits, these are the people I work with,
> these are the systems I use, and this is what I need to learn next."

## Essential systems & access

### The systems you will use

Different roles use different tools. Your exact set depends on your
role, team and location.

You may encounter systems for: - HR and employee information, -
attendance or leave, - expenses, - communication, - email/calendars, -
learning, - project management, - development, - documentation, -
customer/project operations.

### Known company tools

Where officially applicable, current onboarding material references: -
**Zoho People** --- employee/HR-related activities - **Zoho Expense**
--- expense-related activities - **Zoho Recruit** ---
recruitment-related activities - **Microsoft Teams** ---
communication/collaboration - **Outlook** --- email/calendar

> Do not assume every employee uses every system. Your team or HR
> communication should tell you which systems apply to your role.

### If something is not working

1.  Check whether the issue is specific to your account/system.
2.  Read the available official instructions.
3.  Ask your immediate team if they know the correct route.
4.  Contact the relevant IT/HR/Admin support channel.
5.  Describe what you were trying to do, what happened and any visible
    error.

Never share passwords or sensitive credentials when requesting help.

# 4. I NEED TO...

Make this a persistent utility and an important part of Start Here.

## Take leave

> Leave is managed through the applicable HRMS process and current Leave
> Policy.
>
> Before applying: 1. Check which leave type applies. 2. Check your
> balance and eligibility. 3. Plan the leave with your reporting manager
> where required. 4. Submit through the applicable HRMS process. 5. Keep
> the approved request/record.
>
> The official Leave Policy is the source for exact eligibility,
> entitlement, notice requirements and other rules.

**Source:** OFFICIAL --- Leave Policy.

## Claim an expense

> Use the current expense process and applicable official travel/expense
> policy.
>
> Keep invoices/supporting documents, check eligibility, use the
> required system, submit within the applicable timeline, and ask
> Finance/Admin if you are unsure.
>
> Do not assume an expense is reimbursable simply because it was
> incurred for work.

## Travel for work

> Before work-related travel, check the current Domestic/International
> Travel Policy as applicable, confirm whether approval is required, use
> the prescribed booking/reimbursement process, retain required
> documents, and follow the latest travel communication.
>
> Do not use a remembered allowance or process from another employee as
> the rule.

## Report a workplace concern

> If something at work makes you uncomfortable, raises a concern, or
> requires formal support, you do not have to solve it alone.
>
> Start with the appropriate official channel: HR/People, your manager
> where appropriate, or the formal mechanism described in the applicable
> policy.
>
> For matters covered by POSH, use the formal process and contacts
> defined in the current POSH Policy.
>
> Do not put sensitive complaint details into a general onboarding form
> or chat.

## Understand my performance process

> Your manager should help you understand your responsibilities,
> priorities, what good work looks like, how feedback is given, and
> where to find the official performance process.
>
> Use the current Performance Management policy/handbook for formal
> rules.
>
> If you are placed under a formal PIP, the official PIP process takes
> precedence. Ask your manager or HR to explain the applicable
> expectations, review points and documentation.

## Refer someone

> If the Employee Referral Policy applies to you, use the official
> referral process rather than sending a CV informally.
>
> Check the current policy for eligibility, submission process,
> conditions and any referral incentive rules.

## Find a policy

> Start with the official source.
>
> This guide explains policies in plain language where useful, but it
> does not replace them.
>
> When you need an exact rule: 1. open the latest official policy, 2.
> check its version/effective date, 3. follow the current requirement,
> 4. ask HR if the wording is unclear.

## Find the right person

Start with: 1. your manager, 2. your immediate team, 3. the relevant
function such as HR, IT, Finance or Admin, 4. the official contact
listed in the applicable policy/communication.

> Asking the right person is part of learning the organisation.

## Meter-to-decision example

### One example of how technology becomes useful

**1. MEASURE** --- A meter records consumption.

**2. CONNECT** --- The meter or connected infrastructure communicates
information.

**3. COLLECT** --- Data reaches systems responsible for storing and
managing it.

**4. VALIDATE** --- Data may be checked for quality, completeness or
unusual conditions.

**5. ANALYSE** --- Software, analytics or AI can identify patterns and
useful signals.

**6. DECIDE** --- People use the information to understand what is
happening and decide what needs attention.

**7. ACT** --- The utility can take an operational or customer-facing
action.

> The technology matters because it connects the physical world to

# 6. HOW WE WORK

## Hero

**Eyebrow:** HOW WE WORK

**Heading:** Learn the everyday rhythm of work.

**Body:**

> Every team has its own details, but good work usually depends on the
> same basics: clear communication, shared context, ownership, respect,
> documentation and timely feedback. This section gives you the common
> foundation. Your manager and team provide the role-specific details.

## Communicate

### Communicate early. Communicate clearly.

> You will often know something your teammate does not know.
>
> Maybe a task is blocked, a requirement is unclear, a deadline is at
> risk, you found a problem, you need information, you made a mistake,
> or you changed something that affects another person.
>
> Do not wait for the perfect moment to communicate.

A useful update answers:

**What happened? What have I done? What is blocked? What do I need? What
happens next?**

Instead of:

> "I am stuck."

Try:

> "I am implementing X. I completed A and B, but I am blocked because I
> do not have access to C. I have checked the available documentation.
> Could you point me to the correct access process?"

## Meetings

### Meetings are for people, not just calendars.

> You do not need to speak in every meeting. You should know why you are
> there.
>
> Before: know the topic, read shared material, prepare questions.
>
> During: listen, avoid interrupting, ask when unclear, stay relevant,
> note decisions.
>
> After: know what was decided, what you own and when you need to act.

If invited to a meeting you do not understand:

> "Could you help me understand what you would like me to contribute to
> this meeting?"

## Documentation

### If it matters, make it findable.

> Documentation can be a clear task description, decision note,
> technical document, meeting note, process explanation, useful comment,
> handover, or short explanation that saves someone else time.
>
> Good documentation helps another person answer:
>
> What is this? Why does it exist? How does it work? What should I do
> next?
>
> Do not document everything just because you can. Document what another
> person is likely to need later.

## Collaborate

### Your team is bigger than your job title.

> Most meaningful work crosses boundaries.
>
> When you need something: 1. explain what you need, 2. explain why, 3.
> provide enough context, 4. give a reasonable timeframe, 5. acknowledge
> the response, 6. close the loop.
>
> Sometimes the most useful response is:
>
> "I do not own this, but I know who might. Let me connect you."

## Ownership

### Ownership does not mean doing everything alone.

> Ownership means staying responsible for moving your work forward.
>
> It can include understanding the requirement, asking questions,
> identifying dependencies, communicating progress, raising risks,
> completing the work, testing/reviewing where applicable, and
> confirming the outcome is usable.
>
> If blocked, ownership does not mean hiding the blocker. It means
> making the blocker visible and taking the next reasonable step.

## Feedback

### Feedback is part of the work.

> Feedback is how you learn what is working and what needs to change.
>
> When receiving feedback: listen fully, ask questions, avoid treating
> every correction as personal criticism, decide what action to take,
> and follow up.
>
> When giving feedback: be specific, talk about the work or behaviour,
> explain the impact, and focus on what could improve.

A useful feedback conversation should leave the other person knowing:

> "What happened, why it matters, and what I can do differently."

## Performance

### Understand what success looks like.

Your manager should help you understand your role, priorities and
expectations.

Ask: - What are my most important responsibilities? - What should I
focus on first? - How will my work be reviewed? - What does good
performance look like? - How often should we discuss progress? - What
should I do when priorities conflict? - What skills should I develop?

For formal rules, use the current Performance Management policy/process.

## If things are not going well

### A difficult period does not have to become a silent period.

> If you are struggling with workload, unclear expectations, a technical
> problem, a communication issue, a missed deadline, or something
> affecting your ability to work, raise it early.
>
> Start with the person best placed to help --- usually your manager or
> immediate team --- unless the nature of the issue requires another
> formal channel.
>
> If a formal PIP applies, follow the official PIP process and ask your
> manager/HR for clarification about applicable expectations and review
> process.

What does the team need from me in the first month? - What is the best
way to ask for help? - What should I read? - What should I understand
before contributing?

### End-state

> "I know who I work with, what my team does, where to find information,
> and what I need to focus on next."

## Days 8--30 --- UNDERSTAND

### Move from knowing names and systems to understanding the work.

Understand: - problem the team solves, - users of the team's output, -
products/platforms/processes, - how work enters, - how work is
reviewed, - dependencies, - domain terms, - quality expectations, -
common mistakes.

Do: - read documentation, - shadow experienced people, - understand one
product/process area, - follow one piece of work where possible, -
understand impact on another team/customer, - learn business
vocabulary, - document useful learning.

Ask: - What is the most important problem this team solves? - Who
depends on our work? - What usually goes wrong? - What does good quality
look like? - What do new people commonly get wrong? - What is easy to
misunderstand about this business?

### End-state

> "This is what my team does, this is why it matters, this is how work
> moves through the team, and this is where my role contributes."

## Days 31--60 --- PARTICIPATE

### Start contributing with context.

Understand: - how priorities are decided, - how work is assigned, - how
quality is checked, - how blockers are handled, - how decisions are
documented, - how work affects others, - where you can act without
waiting for every instruction.

Do: - own an appropriate task/deliverable, - communicate progress, -
raise blockers, - ask for feedback, - improve a small process/document
if useful, - learn from outside your role, - understand a
customer/business use case.

Ask: - What am I doing well? - Where am I still creating unnecessary
dependency? - What should I become more independent at? - What skill
would make me more useful? - Where can I improve
communication/documentation?

### End-state

> "I can take a piece of work, understand what is expected, communicate
> clearly, get help when needed, and carry it through to an outcome."

## Days 61--90 --- CONTRIBUTE

### Become someone the team can rely on.

Understand: - where you create value, - what the team relies on you
for, - skills to deepen, - relationships important to work, - what to
learn next, - how your work connects to wider goals.

Do: - own appropriate work, - suggest improvements, - share useful
knowledge, - build relationships, - ask for a 90-day feedback
conversation, - define three development priorities, - identify areas
for greater independence.

Ask: - What should I continue? - What should I change? - What should I
learn next? - Where can I take more ownership? - What would make me a
stronger contributor over the next six months?

### End-state

> "I understand my role, my team and the business context well enough to
> contribute with increasing ownership, and I know what I need to learn
> next."

## 30 / 60 / 90 reflection

### Around Day 30

Ask yourself: - What do I understand now that I did not understand on
Day 1? - Who are the people I rely on? - What is still confusing? - What
should I learn next?

Ask your manager: - Am I focusing on the right things? - What should I
improve? - What should I understand better?

### Around Day 60

Ask yourself: - What can I now do independently? - Where do I still need
frequent help? - What have I learned about the business? - What process
or habit could I improve?

Ask your manager: - Where am I becoming stronger? - Where should I take
more ownership? - What skill should I focus on next?

### Around Day 90

Ask yourself: - Can I explain what my team does? - Do I understand where
my work fits? - Do I know how to get help? - What am I now responsible
for? - What do I want to become better at over the next six months?

Ask your manager: - What should I continue? - What should I change? -
Where can I contribute more? - What should my next development priority
be?

# 16. APPENDIX --- GLOSSARY

### AMI --- Advanced Metering Infrastructure

A system of smart meters, communications and supporting technology that
enables utilities to collect and manage meter information remotely.

### Smart Meter

A meter capable of collecting and communicating information digitally,
supporting more frequent or remote measurement than traditional manual
meter reading.

### IoT --- Internet of Things

Connected physical devices that can collect, communicate or act on data.

### Smart Grid

A more connected and data-driven electricity network that uses digital
technologies to improve monitoring, operation and decision-making.

### Utility

An organisation or service provider responsible for essential
infrastructure or services such as electricity, water or gas.

### BESS --- Battery Energy Storage System

A system that stores electrical energy in batteries and makes it
available later when required.

### e-Mobility

Transportation based on electric vehicles and the infrastructure,
software and services that support them.

### Analytics

The process of examining data to understand patterns, performance or
useful information.

### AI --- Artificial Intelligence

Technology that enables systems to perform tasks involving capabilities
such as pattern recognition, prediction or decision support.

### Energy Transition

The broader shift toward energy systems that are cleaner, more
efficient, more connected and increasingly integrated with renewable
energy, storage, electrification and digital technology.

### EaaS --- Energy as a Service

A model in which energy-related solutions or outcomes are provided and
managed as a service.

### Endpoint

A connected device or system that participates in a network or digital
environment.
